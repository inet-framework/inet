IEEE 802.11 HT support: capabilities, associations and rate selection

The IEEE 802.11 model now negotiates High Throughput (HT, 802.11n) operation. Management models and validates the HT Capabilities and HT Operation elements. The local HT capabilities follow the channel widths of the transmitter and the receiver and the number of antenna streams, and an HT transmitter and receiver of incompatible types stop the run with an error that explains the problem. The mixed HT mode set has all MCS 0 to 31 for 20 and 40 MHz with both guard intervals. DCF and HCF select their rates from the negotiated MCS, channel width, guard interval and streams, with a fallback to legacy rates.

Association and reassociation are now transactions. The AP commits an association, with its AID and HT state, only after the station acknowledged the response, so an association whose response fails is not established. A station whose reassociation fails returns to the channel of its old AP. The Beacons and the Probe Responses of an AP advertise the primary channel and the HT operation of the MIB. An associated station refreshes its HT state from the Beacons of its AP, and it checks the state again after a change of the channel or the band during the run.

ac interfaces now use the HT capabilities and HT management together with VHT and legacy OFDM. The advertised and negotiated HT operation stays limited to 20 MHz and four spatial streams. The added HT modes can change the ACK and CTS candidates and the steps of adaptive rate control. An ac AP needs a band with standard channel conversion: the new band "5 GHz (mapped 20 MHz)" gives the standard channels 36, 40, 44 and 48 for HT management, and the supplied ac configurations now use it at 5180 MHz. This change of the frequency can change propagation results and fingerprints. The band does not enable 40 MHz HT operation, and it is not a complete model of a regulatory domain. See the migration guide and the IEEE 802.11 chapter of the User's Guide.

The timing follows the standard more closely. The HT duration at 2.4 GHz includes the signal extension of 6 us. Response deadlines and TXOP timing use the HT and VHT receive-start delay of 24 us; before, a legacy mode at the start of a mixed mode set could give the timing. HT Operation keeps the RIFS Mode, and the MIB permits RIFS under unprotected HT operation and clears the permission for VHT. Rx detects a permitted RIFS from local HT receptions with the same transmitter and receiver addresses, when the preceding frame needs no SIFS response, has signal extension evidence and the BSS permits RIFS at the start of the reception; non-QoS configurations give no RIFS evidence. INET has no RIFS transmission scheduler. A sender estimates a response from the HT capabilities of the recipient; before, it used the wrong peer. These corrections can change airtime, response reservations, timeouts, TXOP admission and simulation results.

This change requires the modification of C++ code that implements IEEE 802.11 modes or rate selection outside INET. IIeee80211Mode has five new pure virtual methods: getModulationClass(), getLegacyPreambleType(), getNonHtReferenceRate(), getVhtMcsIndex() and isVhtShortGuardInterval(); Ieee80211ModeBase gives default values for subclasses (unknown values, and -1 and false for the VHT queries), and an unsupported reference rate is bps(NaN). A custom mode must also give its nominal signal extension, and Ieee80211ModeInd gives the received extension, or a negative value when it is not available. The response queries of IRateSelection and IQosRateSelection take a ResponseQueryRole: PREDICT for the estimate of a sender, RESPOND for the response of a recipient. See the migration guide.

The HT capabilities, the association transactions and the HT timing were contributed by Miguel González-López.
